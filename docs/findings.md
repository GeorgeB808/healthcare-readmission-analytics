
* Primary diagnosis groups demonstrated meaningful variation in observed 30-day readmission rates. Circulatory conditions represented the largest volume, with 30,336 encounters and 3,474 readmissions. Diabetes had an observed readmission rate of 12.98%, approximately 1.82 percentage points above the overall dataset rate of 11.16%. Injury/Complications also showed an elevated rate of 12.25%. These findings suggest that high-volume circulatory conditions and higher-rate groups such as diabetes warrant further investigation when prioritizing quality-improvement efforts.
* Observed 30-day readmission rates generally increased with length of stay through approximately 10 days, rising from 8.18% among one-day encounters to 14.35% among ten-day encounters. The pattern suggests that length of stay may serve as a useful segmentation variable for further investigation, although the relationship should not be interpreted as causal because length of stay may reflect underlying patient complexity and clinical severity.
* Emergency admissions represented the largest admission category, accounting for 53,990 encounters and 6,221 observed 30-day readmissions. The emergency admission readmission rate was 11.52%, compared with 10.39% for elective admissions and 11.18% for urgent admissions. Because emergency encounters represent more than half of the dataset, they represent an important population for further quality-improvement investigation.
* Prior inpatient utilization showed a strong observed relationship with 30-day readmission. Patients with no prior inpatient visits had an 8.44% observed readmission rate, compared with 12.92% among patients with one prior visit and 17.43% among patients with two prior visits. The rate increased to 21.36% among patients with 3–4 prior visits and 36.41% among those with 5 or more prior visits. This pattern suggests that prior inpatient utilization may be an important segmentation variable for identifying populations that warrant further quality-improvement investigation. However, the relationship should not be interpreted as causal, and estimates for the highest-utilization group should be considered alongside its smaller sample size.
